Frequently Asked Questions

Practical, non-sugarcoated guidance for living with an electric car when you lack private off-street parking.

A Garage Orphan is an EV driver or prospective electric car owner who resides in an apartment, condominium, co-op, or urban rowhouse without access to a private garage or assigned carport with a dedicated electrical outlet.

Over 40% of US households (and upwards of 60% in high-density European and Asian cities) fall into this category. Traditionally, EV adoption messaging assumed single-family suburban homeownership with a 240V garage outlet. Garage orphans navigate this gap through curbside Level 1 charging, workplace chargers, public DC Fast Charging hubs, and community peer-to-peer charging networks.

Right-to-Charge laws are statutes enacted by state and provincial governments that make it illegal for an HOA, condo board, or landlord to outright ban a tenant or unit owner from installing an EV charging station at their deeded or assigned parking space.

Currently, states including California (Civ. Code § 4781), Colorado, Florida, Hawaii, New York, New Jersey, Oregon, Virginia, and Washington have established Right-to-Charge laws. Under these rules, as long as the resident pays for installation, sub-metering, and insurance, the board cannot enact unreasonable hurdles or arbitrary rejections. Our Free Request Kit contains specific state-by-state statutory citations.

Critical Safety Note: Never use cheap thin household extension cords. Always use a dedicated 10-AWG commercial outdoor rated cable, verify the outlet has GFCI protection, and use an ADA rubber curb ramp if crossing a public sidewalk to prevent tripping hazards.

P2P charging platforms (such as EVmatch, WattShare, and neighborhood driveway co-ops) function like an "Airbnb for EV chargers." Homeowners with private driveways and Level 2 chargers rent out access to their equipment during working hours or overnight.

For an urban apartment dweller who lives two blocks away, booking a neighbor's driveway twice a week provides reliable Level 2 overnight charging at a fraction of the cost of public DC fast chargers, while generating passive income for the host homeowner.

Modern EV battery management systems (BMS) with liquid cooling handle fast charging well, but you can minimize long-term degradation by following the 20%–80% rule: stop fast charging at 80% where charging speeds taper off and heat accumulates most.

Always allow the vehicle to pre-condition the battery pack before plugging into a high-amperage stall, and try to supplement fast charging with slower Level 2 "opportunity charging" while grocery shopping, watching movies, or exercising at the gym.
